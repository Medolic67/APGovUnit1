5 ideals of democracy

Limited government

Power restricted by laws or a constitution.

Natural rights

Life, liberty, property; no government may take them.

Popular sovereignty

Power comes from the consent of the people.

Republicanism

Governing through elected representatives.

Social contract

We consent to be governed if our rights are protected.

Purpose of government (Statue of Liberty slide): balance liberty and order, and limit government to protect individual rights.

Power vs. authority

Power

The ability to get things done, rightful or not.

Authority

The right to use power. It makes power rightful.

Legitimate power

Conferred by law or constitution, democratic, and widely accepted.

Federalism in one picture

Definition

A written Constitution divides government power on a territorial basis: one national government, many state governments.

🏛️ Delegated / Enumerated

The feds. Written in Art. I, Sec. 8.

Coin moneyCommerceMilitary
Implied powers come from the Necessary & Proper (Elastic) Clause.

🏘️ Reserved

The states (10th Amendment).

Health, education, welfareMarriageSchoolsDrinking ageGambling

🤝 Concurrent

Both, separately and at the same time.

TaxesCrimes & punishmentsRoads

Full Faith & Credit

States respect each other’s records and rulings.

Supremacy Clause

The Constitution is “the Supreme Law of the Land.”

10th Amendment

Powers not delegated to the U.S. nor prohibited to states are reserved to the states or the people.

Two cases about the limits of Congress

McCulloch v. Maryland · 1819
Questions
Could Congress create the Second Bank? Could Maryland tax it?
Ruling
Unanimous: yes, and no.

Why: Necessary & Proper lets Congress create a bank to carry out taxing, debt, borrowing powers.
Takeaway: broad reading of the Elastic Clause expands Congress.

U.S. v. Lopez · 1995
Question
Is the 1990 Gun-Free School Zones Act beyond the Commerce Clause?
Ruling
Yes.

Why: gun possession near a school isn’t economic activity affecting interstate commerce.
Takeaway: the Commerce Clause has limits.

Commerce Clause: Congress may “regulate commerce with foreign nations and among the several states.” Slide asks: child labor laws? Violence Against Women Act? Affordable Care Act? Gun control? What counts as interstate commerce?

🍰 Dual (layer cake)

🤝 Cooperative

Governments work together, often with conditions. Example: highway money if states enforce the drinking age.

The pattern of spending, taxing and grants in the system.

Clinton v. City of New York · 1998

Can Congress give the President a line-item veto? No (6–3).

Assumed to be the case paired with Federalist 51. See the note at the bottom if your teacher meant a different one.

What happened

1
The law
Line Item Veto Act of 1996 lets the President “cancel” items in spending and limited-tax-benefit bills after signing.
➜
2
The cancellations
In 1997 Clinton cancels provisions, including a New York Medicaid-related benefit and an Idaho potato growers’ tax break.
➜
3
The lawsuit
New York City and potato growers sue.
➜
4
The ruling
Supreme Court, 6–3 (Justice Stevens): unconstitutional.

Why it fails

Presentment Clause

Art. I, Sec. 7: the President can only sign or veto a whole bill.

Bicameralism

Cancelling parts of a law amounts to amending or repealing it without both houses.

Dissent

Scalia, O’Connor and Breyer: this is ordinary executive discretion over spending Congress may delegate.

How it connects to Federalist 51

“A will of its own”

Each branch must stay independent. Congress can’t hand the President a lawmaking power.

Ambition vs. ambition

Here Congress gave power away. The Court acted as the check Madison wanted.

Separation vs. checks

A veto is a legitimate check (all-or-nothing). Line-item cancellation lets the President write law.

The reverse danger

Madison feared the legislature dominating; this case shows executive growth.
Different case? If your teacher meant New York v. United States (1992): Congress can’t “commandeer” state legislatures to carry out federal programs (the radioactive-waste “take title” provision). It’s a 10th Amendment and federalism case, and explains why states can legalize marijuana even though federal law bans it: states needn’t enforce federal law, though the Supremacy Clause keeps it in force.

📎 Categorical grants

“Comes with strings attached”

  • Only for specific purposes
  • Awarded to governmental applicants who meet qualifications by a pre-defined formula
  • More common

Formula can use:

PopulationPovertyPer capita incomeUnemploymentPublic school enrollment

Examples:

Head StartFood StampsAsbestos Abatement
VS

🧱 Block grants

“Here’s the money, you figure it out”

  • Given automatically for broad programs
  • Less oversight from the federal government
  • Flexibility for each state

Examples:

Temporary Assistance for Needy FamiliesHomeland Security / Critical Infrastructure Upgrades

  • Decentralized, so performance is hard to measure and officials hard to hold accountable
  • Deck claim: research generally finds them ineffective at reaching policy goals

The feds can’t simply command the states, but they can attach conditions to money. Congress gets its policy; states keep the choice.

The Supreme Court on strings

Beyond your slides · general knowledge
South Dakota v. Dole · 1987

Upheld Congress withholding a small share of federal highway funds from states that kept the drinking age below 21.

Conditions must serve the general welfare, be clearly stated, relate to the purpose of the funds, and not be unconstitutional themselves or coercive.

NFIB v. Sebelius · 2012

Held that Congress went too far by threatening states’ existing Medicaid money if they refused to expand Medicaid. That was coercion, not a choice.

Takeaway: there is a limit to how heavy the strings can be.

Why grants matter for the rest of the unit

Brutus 1

Feared the federal taxing power would swallow up the states’. Grants are that power at work.

Articles

Had no taxing power, so it could only ask states for money. The Constitution fixed that (Art. I, Sec. 8).

10th Amendment

Reserved powers stay with states, yet money lets Congress influence them anyway.

Fed 51

“Double security”: grants are one way the two layers of government negotiate.

Anti-commandeering

Congress can’t order state governments around (New York v. United States), but it can pay them to cooperate.

16th Amendment

The federal income tax gave Washington the revenue to fund grants at scale.

Federalists vs. Anti-Federalists

Federalists

Fed 10, Fed 51
  • Articles too weak; replace them
  • Big republic dilutes faction
  • Fear: instability, majority tyranny
  • Protect liberty by structure
  • Bill of rights not needed at first
VS

Anti-Federalists

Brutus 1, Federal Farmer
  • Fix the Articles, don’t consolidate
  • Only small republics work
  • Fear: distant, elite national tyranny
  • Protect liberty with written rights and state power
  • Wanted a bill of rights before ratifying

Legacy: Federalists → the Constitution’s design. Anti-Federalists → the Bill of Rights and 10th Amendment.

Thesis templates

Because-because

[Position], because [reason 1 from Document A] and [reason 2 from Document B or a case].

Although

Although [other side’s best point], [your position], because [your strongest reason].

Example (grants)

Congress should rely mainly on categorical grants, because the Articles of Confederation show that a national government without real fiscal power cannot get states to act, and conditions on money let Congress protect national goals without commanding states.
